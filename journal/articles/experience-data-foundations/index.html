Building useful experience data foundations

A data foundation is valuable when people can understand it, trust it and use it to answer meaningful questions.

By Sainath Revankar · Calculating reading time · Publication date: add before publishing

Customer data platforms and analytics tools can make a connected view possible, but technology alone does not create one. The foundation is the set of shared decisions that make data understandable and dependable across collection, governance, activation and analysis.

Start with the decisions, not the schema

Before defining events or profile attributes, identify the decisions teams need to make. A product team may need to understand feature adoption; an analytics team may need a consistent definition of a conversion; a marketing team may need to determine whether a person is eligible for a particular experience.

Writing these questions down helps distinguish data that is genuinely useful from data that is merely available. It also exposes differences in terminology early, while they are still inexpensive to resolve.

Give events a consistent shape

Event data benefits from a clear contract: what happened, when it happened, where it happened and which contextual details are required to interpret it. A common event structure makes data easier to validate and reuse across collection endpoints and downstream consumers.

Document required fields, allowed values and ownership. Then define how the implementation behaves when a field is missing, delayed or invalid. A schema is more than a technical artifact; it is an agreement between the people producing and consuming the data.

Make identity and consent explicit

Identity stitching and consent are foundational design concerns, not settings to postpone until activation. Teams should understand which identifiers are collected, what they represent, how they are governed and under what conditions they may be used.

Keep consent signals connected to the systems and workflows that depend on them, and make the rules understandable to the teams responsible for implementation and oversight.

Design for quality and change

Data quality improves when validation is part of the delivery process. Useful checks can confirm that required fields are present, values follow expected formats and key events arrive at reasonable rates. Establish owners and a path for resolving failures so that monitoring leads to action.

Finally, version shared contracts and communicate breaking changes. Data models evolve as products and customer journeys change; a maintainable foundation makes those changes visible rather than surprising downstream users.

A practical starting point

  • List the business and customer questions the data should support.
  • Agree on a small, shared vocabulary for key events and attributes.
  • Document identity, consent, ownership and permitted use.
  • Validate data quality continuously and assign issue ownership.
  • Make changes traceable with clear versioning and communication.

The most useful data foundation is not necessarily the most elaborate one. It is the one that aligns teams around a clear purpose and makes dependable use of data easier over time.
